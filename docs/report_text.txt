Abstract
Access to safe drinking water remains a critical public health concern across rural India,
particularly in Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, where chemical and microbiological contamination of
groundwater sources affects millions of households. Despite the Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM) generating
extensive water quality testing data at habitation level, this information remains largely inaccessible to
local district and block-level officials responsible for prioritising remediation. This project presents
WaterTriage, an independently reproducible, open triage system that ingests publicly available JJM water
quality data and processes it through a backend pipeline built on Python, FastAPI, SQLAlchemy, and
PostgreSQL. A contamination severity scoring engine evaluates each water source against Bureau of
Indian Standards (BIS) 10500 permissible limits, applying differential health-risk weights and an acutehazard escalation rule that prevents severe contaminants such as arsenic and E. coli from being diluted by
compliant aesthetic parameters. The system was tested against a canonical corpus of 1,741 live
laboratory reports (966 from Uttar Pradesh, 775 from Bihar) and 100,690 historical contamination
records. Under the baseline tested-weight composite score at the IS 10500:2012 iron limit of 0.3 mg/L,
the corpus yields 89 Critical, 392 High, 28 Medium, and 1,232 Low samples, with a higher mean score in
Bihar (18.36) than Uttar Pradesh (12.68) despite medians of 0.00 (Bihar) and 4.08 (Uttar Pradesh).
However, median scores reveal that Bihar's elevated mean is substantially driven by a concentrated
cluster of single-parameter iron panels in Madhepura district rather than uniformly elevated risk:
those 70 samples score 100.0 by construction when normalized over tested weights alone, and all 876
raw WQMIS iron records print a lab-recorded limit of 1.0 mg/L rather than the 0.3 mg/L BIS text value.
Re-scoring under the 1.0 mg/L lab limit shifts bands to 5 Critical, 139 High, 30 Medium, and 1,567 Low
(reversing state means to UP 9.99 > Bihar 1.58), while normalizing against total possible weight across
all 10 BIS parameters (TOTAL_BIS_WEIGHT = 6.0, bands 40/20/10) eliminates single-parameter inflation
(3 Critical, 54 High, 125 Medium, 1,559 Low; UP mean 4.72 > Bihar 2.32). State comparisons are
therefore anchored on medians and panel-independent parameter exceedance rates. Both findings are
reported transparently as disclosed limitations of panel composition rather than general contamination
claims. The system exposes ranked, filterable
intervention priority lists through a REST API and an interactive dashboard, demonstrating that
independently reproducible analytics built on government open data can support ground-level water
safety planning without requiring privileged access to internal government systems.
Keywords: Water Quality; Jal Jeevan Mission; Contamination Severity Scoring; BIS 10500;
FastAPI; Intervention Prioritization

1. Introduction
Access to safe and adequate drinking water is a fundamental determinant of public health, yet a
substantial proportion of India's rural population continues to rely on groundwater sources carrying
both chemically-derived and microbiological contamination. Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, situated along
the Indo-Gangetic plain, show elevated arsenic and fluoride readings in the WQMIS dataset, compounded
by nitrate and microbiological contamination from inadequate sanitation infrastructure. The
Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM) has generated one of the largest water quality testing datasets in the country, yet
this data remains consolidated within the Water Quality Management Information System (WQMIS), a
reporting layer designed for national and state-level monitoring rather than direct, actionable use by
district and block-level officials.
This project is motivated by the observation that publicly accessible JJM data can be
independently processed to help close this accessibility gap without requiring modification to, or
privileged access within, existing government systems. The objective is to design and implement
WaterTriage: a reproducible system that ingests JJM water quality data for Uttar Pradesh and Bihar,
scores each source against BIS 10500 limits, and produces a ranked, filterable list of intervention
priorities. The two states were selected for both practical feasibility and their genuinely distinct
contamination geographies, offering a meaningful basis for comparative analysis.

2. Related Work
2.1 Government Water Quality Monitoring Infrastructure. WQMIS, established under JJM by
the Department of Drinking Water and Sanitation, consolidates water quality test results at state and
district laboratories. Published accounts describe this infrastructure as operating primarily for
programme-level monitoring rather than generating ranked, ground-level intervention priorities for block
or panchayat-level functionaries.
2.2 Groundwater Contamination in the Middle Gangetic Plain. Hydrogeochemical research has
documented severe arsenic and fluoride contamination across this region, with arsenic concentrations
reported up to several thousand μg/L in parts of the middle Gangetic floodplain and fluoride
concentrations up to 12 mg/L in affected Bihar districts, substantially exceeding BIS permissible limits.
2.3 Water Quality Index and Contamination Scoring Approaches. Conventional Water Quality
Index (WQI) methodologies are known to suffer from an "eclipsing" effect, whereby a single highly toxic
parameter exceeding safe limits can be masked by a composite score dominated by compliant parameters.
Reviews of WQI methodology find no consensus on universal parameter weighting, concluding that
weighting should be adapted to local health priorities rather than treated as fixed across contexts.
2.4 Positioning of This Project. WaterTriage applies a BIS 10500-grounded, severity-weighted
scoring approach explicitly designed to avoid the eclipsing flaw, to JJM monitoring data for Uttar

Pradesh and Bihar, with the goal of producing actionable output that existing government reporting
systems do not directly provide to local stakeholders.

3. Methodology
3.1 System Architecture. WaterTriage is implemented as a four-stage pipeline: acquisition,
storage, enrichment, and serving. Data is acquired from public WQMIS reports and cached locally,
persisted in a normalized PostgreSQL schema, enriched with per-record severity scores, and served
through a REST API and browser dashboard.

3.3 Contamination Severity Scoring. Each parameter reading is converted to a severity value
between 0 and 1 by linear interpolation between the BIS acceptable and permissible limits. During
development, iron and nitrate have no permissible ceiling defined by BIS IS 10500:2012 (both carry
the explicit note "No relaxation"), so any exceedance of the acceptable limit saturates directly to maximum
severity (a binary rather than graded response). Bis IS 10500:2012 Table 2 specifies iron's acceptable
limit at 0.3 mg/L with "No relaxation"; this is the limit enforced by the scoring engine
(scoring.py: acceptable = 0.3, permissible = None). In the raw WQMIS laboratory records, all 876 iron
readings (450 Bihar, 426 Uttar Pradesh) print an acceptable limit of 1.0 mg/L from an earlier relaxation
criterion — a label conflict between the standard and the lab, not a corrected value. The composite risk
score (CRS) for a sample is the
weighted mean of severity across tested parameters, scaled to 0–100, using health-risk weights (arsenic =
1.0, E. coli = 0.9, total coliform = 0.9, fluoride = 0.8, nitrate = 0.7, sulphate = 0.5, iron = 0.4, turbidity
= 0.3, TDS = 0.3, pH = 0.2; TOTAL_BIS_WEIGHT = 6.0). Band thresholds are Critical (≥40), High (20–39),
Medium (10–19), Low (<10); the earlier ≥75/50–74/25–49/<25 thresholds were unreachable under the acute-hazard
escalation logic (max achievable ≈ 48), so the bands were lowered to fit the achievable score range. A
band-escalation rule additionally forces a minimum "High" classification for any parameter reaching full
severity, and a "Critical" classification for acute-hazard parameters (E. coli, arsenic, total coliform)
irrespective of the numeric composite score. Based on CRS, each sample is classified as Critical (≥40),
High (20–39), Medium (10–19), or Low/Safe (<10).
3.4 Recurrence Detection. A recurrence classification module blends historical registry presence with
current exceedance status, producing verdicts of chronic-hotspot, recurring, watchlist, or no-history.
Because the village-identifier schemes of the two sources are disjoint, the module is deployed at
district level (backend/app/engine/recurrence_service.py): a district's historical parameters and years
are aggregated from the registry, its current exceedances from live samples, and the classification is
computed per district-parameter pair. Across the 32 districts that hold both registry history and at
least two current live samples, the module returns 6 chronic-hotspot, 13 recurring, and 13 watchlist
verdicts. Live sample-to-village recurrence is unavailable for the same identifier reason and is
disclosed in Section 6. The three highest-scoring districts are Kishanganj, Sheikhpura and Madhepura
(each 100.0), followed by Varanasi (66.67) and Basti (58.33); Agra scores 46.88 under the 8-parameter
set and 37.50 once sulphate and total coliform are included; Baghpat 34.38/32.50; Lucknow 20.31/21.25.
3.5 Data Acquisition. The primary acquisition channel is a crawler against WQMIS's public,
unauthenticated report-printing endpoint, operating with caching and resume checkpoints. A historical
Quality-Affected Habitations registry (2009–2012, 100,690 events) was loaded as a supplementary
source.

Bihar's mean composite risk score (18.36) exceeds Uttar Pradesh's (12.68) under Model v1;
however, Bihar's median score is 0.00, compared to 4.08 for Uttar Pradesh, indicating that more
than half of Bihar's scored samples fall in the Low/Safe band. This divergence between mean and
median is explained by a specific data artifact: 70 of the 89 Model-v1 Critical-band samples (78.7%)
originate from Madhepura district alone, where all 70 samples were tested under a single-parameter
panel for iron only. Under the tested-weight scoring rule, any iron reading at or above the
permissible limit saturates to maximum severity, and because no other parameters were tested for
these samples, the composite score for each equals 100 by construction. Under Model v2's
total-weight normalization, a single-parameter iron exceedance scores 0.4/6.0 x 100 = 6.67 (Low), so
the same 70 samples fall to Low and the state mean ordering reverses (UP 4.72 > Bihar 2.32). This is
disclosed explicitly as a panel-composition limitation rather than evidence of uniformly severe
contamination across Bihar, and is surfaced to dashboard consumers via the /api/meta endpoint's
documented limitations.

4.4 Escalation Rule Validation. During testing, synthetic and live cases confirmed that the bandescalation rule (Section 3.3) correctly reclassifies samples with acute hazards (E. coli presence or arsenic
above 0.01 mg/L) to Critical band even where the arithmetic composite score alone would classify the sample
as Low or Medium, confirming the rule functions as intended to prevent the eclipsing failure mode
discussed in Section 2.3. The arsenic escalation threshold is 0.01 mg/L (IS 10500:2012 Table 3, item x),
which is stricter than the table's 0.05 mg/L permissible column; Amendment 1 (2015) removed that
relaxation. Across all 259 arsenic-tested samples (230 Bihar, 29 Uttar Pradesh), the maximum reading is
0.009 mg/L with 0 exceedances and 150 recorded zeros, so zero samples fall between the 0.01 and 0.05
mg/L bands and the threshold choice does not affect any corpus result.
4.5 District-Level Recurrence Results. The recurrence module was run at district level against the
deduplicated 2009–2012 registry (100,690 distinct events across 14,006 villages in 93 districts) and the
canonical 1,741-sample corpus. Thirty-two districts hold both registry history and at least two current
live samples, containing 873 live samples (419 UP, 454 Bihar) and 21,814 historical events. Under the
8-parameter set in force when the weekly reports were written, the module returns 6 chronic-hotspot,
13 recurring, and 13 watchlist districts; the 6 chronic-hotspot districts are Kishanganj, Sheikhpura,
Madhepura, Varanasi, Basti and Agra — each holds at least one parameter exceeding in the live corpus
that the registry also records as affected across multiple years. Adding sulphate and total coliform to the scored set leaves the
verdict counts unchanged but shifts individual scores (Agra 46.88 -> 37.50, Baghpat 34.38 -> 32.50,
Lucknow 20.31 -> 21.25).
The historical registry confirms Khokha village (Purnia district,
Bihar) as the single most frequently recorded location for iron-related events in the 2009–2012 registry,

with 166 recorded events — the highest of any village in the historical dataset. However, because the
production water_samples table and the historical_contamination registry use disjoint village identifier
schemes, this historical finding could not be linked to a corresponding live risk score for the same village

figure is reported here as a registry-level finding only, and the identifier-mapping gap is recorded as an

5. Discussion
The results in Section 4.3 illustrate why reporting both mean and median statistics, together with
panel-composition context, is necessary for an honest comparative analysis: a mean-only reading would
have suggested Bihar carries substantially higher contamination risk than Uttar Pradesh across the board,
when the more complete picture is that a small, structurally distinct cluster of single-parameter samples
accounts for the great majority of that gap. This is treated in this report as a methodological finding about
the dataset's composition, not a claim about underlying contamination incidence rates between the two
states, which this sample is not equipped to estimate without correcting for panel-size variation.
The correction to the iron/nitrate scoring logic (Section 3.3) was itself a significant finding: the
original binary-saturation behaviour for parameters lacking an explicit permissible limit caused
disproportionate Critical-band inflation, which was only identified through systematic re-verification of
the scoring engine against its own BIS 10500 justification. This underscores the value of treating a
scoring model's assumptions as testable claims rather than fixed infrastructure.
The three-way scoring audit (Section 4.2, Table 1a) makes the same point structurally. Because the
baseline model normalized by tested weights only, a sample tested for a single parameter could reach the
maximum score of 100 on that parameter alone: 75 of the 1,741 samples score exactly 100.00 under Model
v1, and every one of them tested a single parameter — iron in all 75 cases. The coverage-adjusted
model normalizes against the total possible BIS weight (6.0), so the same samples score 0.4/6.0 x 100 =
6.67 and land in Low, while the previously near-empty Medium band populates (28 -> 125 samples, 1.6% ->
7.2%). Agra illustrates the same effect from the opposite direction: its 43 samples are a multi-parameter
fluoride cluster (41 of 43 test five BIS parameters; 31 have fluoride as their worst-contributing
parameter; mean CRS 60.13 under Model v1), and Agra also holds all seven UP sulphate readings that
exceed the 400 mg/L permissible limit.
The withdrawal of the Khokha chronic-hotspot claim (Section 4.5) reflects the same discipline:
rather than retaining a compelling but unverifiable narrative example, the identifier-mismatch limitation
is disclosed directly, and the historical finding is reported at the scope it can actually support.

6. Limitations
This work is bounded by several limitations that should inform how its findings are used. First,
the 1,741 live laboratory reports represent a sample drawn from targeted WQMIS identifier ranges rather
than a spatially uniform census of either state; sample density by district reflects crawler coverage, not
necessarily underlying population-level contamination burden. Second, as discussed in Section 4.3, a
subset of Critical-band samples originate from single-parameter test panels (iron only), which
mechanically produces maximum severity scores independent of a source's overall water quality and
should not be read as representative of multi-parameter contamination severity. Third, live linkage

between the historical contamination registry and current water_samples records could not be established
due to disjoint village identifier schemes between the two sources, limiting recurrence/trend
classification to registry-level findings rather than sample-linked verdicts for this reporting period.
Fourth, a temporal discontinuity exists between the historical registry (2009–2012) and current WQMIS
testing cycles (2022), so historical recurrence indicators describe district-level historical patterns
rather than real-time contamination status for any specific habitation.
